Business Situation · Pricing Strategy

Should we cut price to win volume—and what happens if the market does not respond?

Pricing decisions can look simple until demand, margins, costs, customer expectations, and competitive reactions move together. A business war game lets management test those interactions before using the market itself as the experiment.

Explore CAEAll Use Cases
Pricing decision
Price move → customer response → competitor response → volume → margin → next move

The decision is dynamic because today's move changes tomorrow's options.

Public market example

PepsiCo's 2026 snack-price reversal shows the trade-off.

Public reporting described PepsiCo cutting prices on several major snack brands by as much as 15% in an effort to revive demand. Months later, North American food volumes were still flat and the company planned partial price increases as cost and margin pressures persisted.

The management problem

Lower prices may recover volume, but only if demand responds enough to offset the lost margin. The outcome also depends on consumer pressure, competitor pricing, input costs, promotions, brand strength, and whether customers come to expect the lower price.

What a pricing war game can test

Alternative price cuts, no-change scenarios, competitor responses, demand sensitivity, margin recovery, promotional support, timing, and the consequences of reversing course if the original move underperforms.
